# Collateral Classification Under UCC Article 9

## Overview

Article 9 of the Uniform Commercial Code classifies collateral into distinct categories because the rules for attachment, perfection, and priority can vary depending on the type of property. Correctly classifying collateral is one of the most important steps in any secured transaction. Misclassification can lead to filing errors, incorrect perfection methods, and loss of priority.

Collateral classifications fall into three broad groups: goods, quasi-tangible property, and intangible property. Each group contains several specific types with their own rules.

## Goods

Goods are tangible, movable personal property. Article 9 subdivides goods into four categories based on how the debtor uses them. The same physical item can fall into different categories depending on context.

### Consumer Goods

Consumer goods are goods used or bought primarily for personal, family, or household purposes. Examples include a family car, household furniture, personal electronics, and clothing.

A security interest in consumer goods can be perfected automatically if it qualifies as a purchase-money security interest (PMSI). This means no financing statement needs to be filed. For example, when a consumer finances the purchase of a refrigerator, the retailer's security interest perfects automatically upon attachment.

### Equipment

Equipment is goods used or bought primarily for use in a business. This is the default category for goods that do not fit into consumer goods, inventory, or farm products. Examples include office computers, manufacturing machinery, company vehicles, medical devices, and restaurant ovens.

Equipment is typically perfected by filing a UCC-1 financing statement. There is no automatic perfection for equipment, even with a PMSI (though a PMSI in equipment receives superpriority if perfected within 20 days of the debtor receiving possession).

### Farm Products

Farm products are goods used or produced in farming operations while in the possession of a debtor engaged in farming. This includes crops, livestock, supplies used in farming, and products of crops or livestock in their unmanufactured state (such as raw milk, eggs, or maple sap).

Once farm products leave the farmer's possession and enter the stream of commerce, they are reclassified as inventory in the hands of the buyer.

### Inventory

Inventory includes goods held for sale or lease, goods furnished under a service contract, raw materials, work in process, and materials used or consumed in a business. Examples include retail merchandise on store shelves, a car dealer's vehicle lot, lumber at a building supply company, and parts awaiting assembly.

Inventory is a critical collateral type for asset-based lending. A PMSI in inventory has superpriority but requires the secured party to file before the debtor receives the inventory and to notify other secured parties of record.

## Quasi-Tangible Property

Quasi-tangible property bridges the gap between purely physical goods and purely intangible rights. These assets have a physical embodiment that represents an underlying right or obligation.

### Instruments

Instruments are negotiable instruments (such as promissory notes and drafts) and other writings that evidence a right to payment of money. A common example is a promissory note signed by a borrower. Instruments can be perfected by filing, but perfection by possession gives priority over a filing-based perfection. This is why lenders typically require physical delivery of promissory notes.

### Documents of Title

Documents of title are documents that in the regular course of business are treated as establishing that the person in possession is entitled to receive, hold, and dispose of the goods covered by the document. Examples include warehouse receipts and bills of lading. These are common in commodity trading and shipping.

### Chattel Paper

Chattel paper is a record or group of records that evidences both a monetary obligation and a security interest in or lease of specific goods. The most common example is an auto loan agreement: the document combines the borrower's promise to pay with the lender's security interest in the vehicle.

Chattel paper can be tangible (physical documents) or electronic. Electronic chattel paper has special perfection rules requiring "control" through a system that maintains a single authoritative copy.

## Intangible Property

Intangible property represents rights that have no physical embodiment. These are among the most economically significant types of collateral in modern secured lending.

### Accounts

Accounts are rights to payment for goods sold, leased, or licensed, or for services rendered, that are not evidenced by chattel paper or an instrument. The most common example is accounts receivable. When a business invoices a customer for delivered goods, the right to collect that invoice is an "account" under Article 9.

Accounts receivable financing (factoring) is one of the largest segments of secured lending. Perfection is by filing a UCC-1.

### Deposit Accounts

Deposit accounts are demand, time, savings, passbook, or similar accounts maintained at a bank. A security interest in a deposit account as original collateral (not proceeds) can only be perfected by "control," which typically means the secured party is the bank where the account is maintained, or the secured party has obtained a control agreement signed by the debtor and the bank.

### General Intangibles

General intangibles is the catch-all category for personal property that does not fit into any other Article 9 classification. It includes intellectual property (patents, trademarks, copyrights), goodwill, software (to the extent it is not embedded in goods), tax refund claims, and payment intangibles.

Payment intangibles are a subcategory of general intangibles in which the principal obligation is a right to payment of money. A security interest in payment intangibles perfects automatically upon attachment if the transaction is a sale of payment intangibles.

### Investment Property

Investment property includes securities (both certificated and uncertificated), securities accounts, security entitlements, commodity contracts, and commodity accounts. This category covers stocks, bonds, mutual fund shares, and brokerage accounts.

Perfection of a security interest in investment property can be achieved by filing, by control, or by delivery (for certificated securities). Control provides superior priority over filing, so secured parties typically seek control agreements with brokers or securities intermediaries.

## Why Classification Matters

Correct classification determines:

- **How to perfect.** Some collateral types require filing, others require possession or control, and some perfect automatically.
- **Where to file.** While most filings are made with the Secretary of State, fixtures require a local filing.
- **Priority rules.** PMSI superpriority rules differ between inventory and non-inventory goods. Control-based perfection defeats filing-based perfection for deposit accounts and investment property.
- **Description requirements.** The collateral description in a financing statement must be adequate for the specific type being described.

A single debtor's property portfolio may span multiple collateral categories. For example, a manufacturing company's assets might include equipment (the machines), inventory (the finished goods), accounts (the invoices for sold goods), general intangibles (the patents), and deposit accounts (the bank accounts). Each requires careful classification and potentially different perfection strategies.
