# How to Conduct a UCC Lien Search

## Why Search

A UCC lien search is one of the most critical steps in commercial due diligence. Before lending money secured by personal property, acquiring a business, or entering into a significant commercial transaction, you need to know what existing security interests encumber the debtor's assets.

Failing to conduct a proper lien search can result in extending credit that is effectively unsecured because another creditor holds a prior perfected security interest. In a bankruptcy, an unsecured or junior creditor may recover pennies on the dollar, or nothing at all. A thorough lien search protects against this risk.

## Where to Search

UCC financing statements are filed at the state level, almost always with the Secretary of State. The correct state for searching depends on the type of debtor:

- **Registered organizations** (corporations, LLCs, LPs): Search in the state where the entity is organized, not where it conducts business. A Delaware LLC operating in California has its UCC filings in Delaware.
- **Individuals**: Search in the state of the individual's principal residence.
- **Foreign entities**: Non-US entities organized in a foreign jurisdiction file in the District of Columbia.

Most Secretaries of State provide online search portals. Many also accept mail-in or in-person search requests. Third-party search services aggregate filings across states and can expedite multi-state searches.

## Search Logic

UCC search systems use "standard search logic" as defined by the International Association of Commercial Administrators (IACA). Understanding how these systems work is critical for conducting effective searches and identifying all relevant filings.

### Exact Match vs. Broad Search

Most state filing offices use a standardized search logic that ignores certain differences between the search query and the filed debtor name:

- **Case insensitivity.** "ACME Corp" and "acme corp" are treated the same.
- **Noise words.** Words like "the," "an," and "a" at the beginning of a name are typically ignored.
- **Punctuation.** Periods, commas, and other punctuation are generally disregarded.
- **Entity endings.** Some systems treat "Inc," "Inc.," "Incorporated," and similar variations as equivalent.

However, the search logic does NOT typically correct for misspellings, transposed letters, or abbreviations. If a filing misspells the debtor's name, a search under the correct name may not return the filing. This is why a seriously misleading name error can render a filing ineffective.

### Best Practices for Searching

- Search the exact legal name of the debtor as shown on organizational documents
- Run additional searches using known trade names, former names, and common abbreviations
- For individuals, search all known name variations (maiden name, middle name variations)
- Check for recent name changes that may not yet be reflected in amended filings

## Interpreting Results

A UCC search will return a list of active financing statements naming the debtor. For each filing, examine:

- **Secured party.** Who holds the security interest? Is it a major bank, an equipment lessor, a factoring company, or a related party?
- **Collateral description.** What property is encumbered? Is it specific ("all equipment located at 500 Industrial Blvd") or blanket ("all assets")?
- **Filing date.** When was the filing made? Earlier filings generally have priority over later ones.
- **Status.** Is the filing active, lapsed, or terminated? Check for continuation statements that extend the filing beyond its initial five-year term.
- **Amendments.** Have there been any UCC-3 amendments? Check for collateral additions, deletions, party changes, or assignments.
- **Correction statements.** Are there any UCC-5 correction statements indicating disputes about the filing?

## Red Flags

Certain patterns in lien search results should trigger heightened scrutiny and additional investigation:

### Multiple Liens

When several creditors hold security interests in the same debtor's assets, it indicates the debtor has leveraged its collateral to obtain multiple lines of credit. This is not inherently problematic but increases credit risk. Pay close attention to the priority ordering of the filings and whether your proposed security interest would be junior to existing ones.

### Blanket Liens

A blanket lien covers "all assets" or "all personal property" of the debtor. This means the secured party claims a security interest in everything the debtor owns. A prior blanket lien makes it extremely difficult for a subsequent lender to obtain a meaningful security position, since the prior lien holder has first priority on all assets. If you discover a blanket lien, determine whether the existing lender will subordinate its interest or carve out specific collateral.

### Expired Filings

A filing that has lapsed (passed its five-year term without a continuation) may indicate either that the underlying debt was repaid or that the secured party made an administrative error. Do not assume a lapsed filing means the collateral is free and clear. Investigate whether the obligation was actually satisfied or whether the creditor simply failed to continue the filing.

### Tax Liens and Judgment Liens

While UCC searches focus on consensual security interests, also check for federal and state tax liens, which are filed separately and have special priority rules. IRS tax liens, in particular, can prime even previously perfected security interests under certain circumstances.

## Due Diligence Best Practices

A thorough lien search process includes:

1. **Search all relevant jurisdictions.** Search the debtor's state of organization and any states where the debtor previously was organized (in case of redomestication).
2. **Search related entities.** If the debtor is a subsidiary, search the parent company and affiliates that may hold collateral.
3. **Verify the debtor's legal name.** Obtain and review the debtor's organizational documents (certificate of formation, articles of incorporation) to confirm the exact legal name.
4. **Check for name changes.** Review the debtor's history for mergers, conversions, or name changes that could affect filings.
5. **Review all filings in detail.** Do not rely solely on the search summary. Pull and read each financing statement, including all amendments.
6. **Document your search.** Retain copies of search results and the date of the search. A certified search from the filing office provides the strongest evidence.
7. **Update before closing.** Conduct a final search immediately before closing the transaction to catch any filings made between your initial search and the closing date.

A comprehensive lien search is not merely a checkbox exercise. It is a critical risk management tool that protects the lender's investment and ensures informed decision-making.
