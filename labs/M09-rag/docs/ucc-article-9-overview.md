# UCC Article 9: Secured Transactions Overview

## What is Article 9?

Article 9 of the Uniform Commercial Code (UCC) is the primary body of law governing secured transactions in personal property throughout the United States. A secured transaction is any transaction in which a creditor (the "secured party") obtains a security interest in personal property owned by the debtor. This security interest gives the creditor a legal claim to specified collateral if the debtor defaults on the underlying obligation, typically a loan or credit agreement.

Article 9 does not govern real estate mortgages, which fall under separate state real property law. Instead, Article 9 covers security interests in personal property and fixtures, including tangible goods, intangible assets, and financial instruments. Every state has adopted some version of Article 9, though minor variations exist between jurisdictions.

The current version, Revised Article 9, took effect in most states on July 1, 2001, and was further amended in 2010. It replaced the original 1972 version and significantly modernized the rules around filing, priority, and the treatment of electronic records.

## Key Concepts

Article 9 revolves around three fundamental concepts: attachment, perfection, and priority. Attachment creates the security interest between the parties. Perfection makes the security interest effective against third parties. Priority determines which creditor wins when multiple parties claim the same collateral.

Understanding these three concepts is essential for anyone working in commercial lending, asset-based finance, or due diligence on business acquisitions.

## Attachment

Attachment is the process by which a security interest becomes enforceable between the debtor and the secured party. For a security interest to attach, three requirements must be satisfied simultaneously:

1. **Value must be given.** The secured party must provide something of value to the debtor. This is almost always a loan, line of credit, or some other form of consideration. A binding commitment to extend credit is sufficient.

2. **The debtor must have rights in the collateral.** The debtor must own the property or have sufficient rights to grant a security interest in it. A debtor cannot encumber property it does not own, though after-acquired property clauses can capture assets the debtor obtains in the future.

3. **A security agreement must exist.** The debtor must authenticate a security agreement that provides a description of the collateral. Under Revised Article 9, the description must "reasonably identify" the collateral but need not be exhaustive. Supergeneric descriptions like "all assets" are permitted in security agreements (but not in financing statements for certain collateral types).

Once all three elements are present, the security interest attaches automatically. No filing or additional formality is required for attachment alone.

## Perfection

Perfection is the step that makes a security interest enforceable against third parties, including other creditors, lien holders, and a bankruptcy trustee. An unperfected security interest is valid between the debtor and secured party, but it is vulnerable to being defeated by competing claims.

The most common method of perfection is filing a UCC-1 financing statement with the appropriate state filing office, typically the Secretary of State in the state where the debtor is organized (for registered organizations) or located (for individuals). The financing statement provides public notice that the secured party claims an interest in the described collateral.

Other methods of perfection include:

- **Possession.** The secured party takes physical possession of the collateral (common for negotiable instruments, certificated securities, and tangible chattel paper).
- **Control.** The secured party obtains "control" over certain types of collateral such as deposit accounts, electronic chattel paper, investment property, and letter-of-credit rights.
- **Automatic perfection.** Certain security interests perfect automatically upon attachment without any filing. The most common example is a purchase-money security interest (PMSI) in consumer goods.

The method of perfection matters because it affects priority. A financing statement is effective for five years from the date of filing and must be continued by filing a UCC-3 continuation statement before expiration.

## Priority Rules

When multiple creditors claim a security interest in the same collateral, Article 9's priority rules determine who gets paid first. The general rules are:

1. **Perfected vs. unperfected.** A perfected security interest has priority over an unperfected one in virtually all cases.

2. **First to file or perfect.** Among perfected security interests, priority generally goes to the party that first filed a financing statement or first perfected, whichever occurred earlier. This is the "first-in-time" rule.

3. **Purchase-money security interest (PMSI) superpriority.** A PMSI in goods other than inventory has priority over a conflicting security interest if the PMSI is perfected within 20 days after the debtor receives possession of the collateral. A PMSI in inventory has superpriority if the purchase-money secured party files before the debtor takes possession and sends notice to holders of conflicting security interests.

4. **Buyers in the ordinary course of business.** A buyer who purchases goods in the ordinary course of business from a seller who deals in goods of that kind takes free of any security interest created by the seller, even if the security interest is perfected and the buyer knows of it.

5. **Lien creditors.** A judicial lien creditor (including a bankruptcy trustee) defeats an unperfected security interest but loses to a security interest perfected before the lien arises.

These priority rules create strong incentives for creditors to perfect their security interests as quickly as possible and to monitor filings by competing creditors.

## Filing Requirements

UCC filings are made at the state level, almost always with the Secretary of State. The key filing rules under Revised Article 9 include:

- **Where to file.** For registered organizations (corporations, LLCs, LPs), file in the state of the debtor's organization. For individuals, file in the state of the debtor's principal residence.

- **What to include.** A financing statement (UCC-1) must contain: (1) the debtor's name, (2) the secured party's name, and (3) a description of the collateral. The debtor's name must exactly match the name on the debtor's organizational documents or driver's license.

- **Name accuracy.** An error in the debtor's name that makes the financing statement "seriously misleading" renders the filing ineffective. Revised Article 9 uses a "standard search logic" test: if a search of the filing office's records under the debtor's correct name would not disclose the financing statement, the name is seriously misleading.

- **Duration.** A UCC-1 financing statement is effective for five years. It must be continued by filing a UCC-3 continuation statement within six months before expiration. If not continued, the filing lapses and the security interest becomes unperfected.

- **Amendments.** Changes to the debtor's name, collateral description, or secured party information require filing a UCC-3 amendment. If the debtor changes its name so seriously that a search under the new name would not find the original filing, the secured party has four months to file an amendment or lose perfection in collateral acquired after the name change.

Proper filing is critical. A lapsed or defective filing can result in loss of priority, leaving the creditor unsecured in a bankruptcy proceeding.
