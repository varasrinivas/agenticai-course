# UCC Filing Procedures — Frequently Asked Questions

**Document Version:** 2024.1
**Last Updated:** February 5, 2024
**Audience:** Filing officers, paralegals, credit analysts, compliance teams

---

## General Filing Questions

### Q1: What is the difference between a UCC-1 and a UCC-3?

A **UCC-1** (Financing Statement) is the original filing that creates the
public record of a security interest. It is filed to perfect a security
interest in personal property collateral.

A **UCC-3** (Financing Statement Amendment) is used for all subsequent
actions against an existing UCC-1 filing. This includes:

- **Continuation:** Extending the filing for another 5 years
- **Amendment:** Changing debtor/secured party names or collateral descriptions
- **Assignment:** Transferring the secured party's interest to another party
- **Termination:** Ending the filing when the debt is satisfied

Think of the UCC-1 as the "birth certificate" of the security interest, and
the UCC-3 as any "life event" that modifies or ends it.

### Q2: How long does a UCC filing last?

A UCC-1 financing statement is effective for **5 years** from the date of
filing. After 5 years, the filing "lapses" and the security interest becomes
unperfected (unless a UCC-3 Continuation is filed).

**Important timing rules:**
- A continuation must be filed within the **6-month window** before the lapse
  date (i.e., between 4 years 6 months and 5 years after filing)
- Filing a continuation BEFORE the 6-month window is ineffective
- Filing a continuation AFTER the lapse date is too late — a new UCC-1 must
  be filed
- Each continuation extends the filing for another 5 years from the current
  lapse date (not from the continuation filing date)

### Q3: How much does it cost to file a UCC?

Filing fees vary by state. Typical ranges:

- **UCC-1 filing:** $10 to $50 per filing (most states: $10-$25)
- **UCC-3 amendment:** Same fee as UCC-1 in most states
- **UCC search:** $5 to $25 per debtor name
- **Certified search:** $15 to $50 per debtor name

Fees can add up for portfolio-wide filings. For example, a lender with 1,000
active loans may spend $10,000-$25,000 annually on continuation filings alone.

Delaware is notably more expensive ($50 per filing) but offers premium
expedited services including 1-hour turnaround for $1,000 and 2-hour
turnaround for $500.

### Q4: Where do I file a UCC financing statement?

**For organizations (corporations, LLCs, LPs, etc.):**
File with the Secretary of State in the state where the organization was
formed (state of incorporation or formation). This is NOT necessarily the
state where the organization does business or where the collateral is located.

Example: A Delaware LLC operating in California with equipment in Texas files
its UCC-1 with the Delaware Secretary of State.

**For individuals:**
File with the Secretary of State in the state where the individual has their
principal residence.

**Exceptions:**
- Fixtures, timber, minerals: File with the county recorder where the real
  property is located
- Transmitting utilities: Special federal filing rules may apply

### Q5: Can I file a UCC against someone who doesn't owe me money?

Generally, no. A UCC filing should only be filed if there is a legitimate
security interest. Filing a fraudulent or unauthorized UCC-1 can result in:

- **Civil liability:** The "debtor" can sue for damages
- **Criminal penalties:** In many states, filing a false lien is a felony
- **Statutory damages:** Some states impose automatic damages (e.g., $500)
  for unauthorized filings
- **Filing office refusal:** Some states have processes to flag and reject
  clearly fraudulent filings (e.g., "sovereign citizen" filings)

However, there are legitimate "precautionary filings" — for example, a
factoring company may file a UCC-1 even if the transaction is technically a
sale of accounts (not a secured loan), just to be safe in case a court later
recharacterizes the transaction.

## Debtor Name Questions

### Q6: How do I determine the correct debtor name?

**For organizations:**
1. Identify the debtor's state of organization (where it was formed)
2. Search the state's business entity database (e.g., Delaware Division of
   Corporations, California bizfileOnline)
3. Use the EXACT legal name as shown in the state database
4. Include the entity suffix exactly as shown (LLC, Inc., Corp., etc.)
5. Do NOT use: trade names, DBA names, former names, or abbreviations

**For individuals:**
1. Obtain the debtor's current, unexpired driver's license from the state of
   their principal residence
2. Use the name exactly as shown on the driver's license
3. If the debtor does not have a driver's license, use the debtor's legal
   name (as shown on passport, Social Security card, or birth certificate)
4. In "safe-harbor" states, either the DL name or legal name is acceptable

### Q7: What happens if I get the debtor name wrong?

An incorrect debtor name can render the filing **"seriously misleading"** and
therefore ineffective. This means:

- The filing may not provide notice to other creditors
- The security interest may not be perfected
- In a bankruptcy, the trustee could avoid (defeat) the security interest
- The secured party could lose its priority position

The test is whether a search under the **correct** debtor name, using the
filing office's **standard search logic**, would disclose the filing with the
incorrect name. If the search WOULD find it, the error is not seriously
misleading. If the search would NOT find it, the filing is ineffective.

**Real-world examples:**
- "Wiersma Inc." filed as "Wiersma" — seriously misleading (missing entity suffix)
- "John Smith" filed as "Jon Smith" — may or may not be seriously misleading
  depending on the filing office's search logic
- "ABC Holdings LLC" filed as "ABC Holdings, LLC" — generally NOT seriously
  misleading (punctuation variation)
- "Acme Corporation" filed as "Acme Corp." — could be seriously misleading
  (abbreviation of entity suffix)

### Q8: The debtor changed its name. What do I need to do?

If the debtor legally changes its name (e.g., corporate name change,
individual name change due to marriage):

1. **Within 4 months:** The existing filing remains effective. File a UCC-3
   amendment to update the debtor name.
2. **After 4 months (without amendment):** The filing becomes ineffective as
   to collateral acquired by the debtor AFTER the name change. It remains
   effective for collateral acquired before the name change.
3. **Best practice:** Monitor debtor name changes and file amendments promptly
   (within 30 days is recommended)

## Amendment and Continuation Questions

### Q9: What is the difference between an amendment and a continuation?

- A **continuation** simply extends the filing's life for another 5 years. It
  does not change any information on the filing. It can only be filed during
  the 6-month window before lapse.

- An **amendment** changes the content of the filing (debtor name, secured
  party name, collateral description). An amendment can be filed at any time
  during the filing's life.

They are filed on the same form (UCC-3) but use different checkboxes.

### Q10: Can I combine a continuation and an amendment on the same UCC-3?

**Yes.** A single UCC-3 can serve as both a continuation AND an amendment.
This is a common practice to save filing fees. Simply check both the
"Continuation" and "Amendment" boxes on the UCC-3 form and include the
amendment information.

However, if the filing is NOT within the continuation window, the continuation
portion will be rejected (or simply ineffective), even if the amendment
portion is accepted.

### Q11: I missed the continuation window. What now?

If the filing has lapsed (the 5-year period has expired without a
continuation being filed):

1. The security interest is now **unperfected** as to third parties
2. You CANNOT revive the lapsed filing — a new UCC-1 must be filed
3. The new filing's priority date will be the date of the NEW filing (you
   lose your original priority position)
4. Other creditors who filed or perfected between the lapse and the new filing
   will have priority
5. A bankruptcy trustee may be able to avoid the security interest if the
   lapse occurred within the preference period (90 days before bankruptcy filing)

**Prevention:** Set up a tickler/calendar system to flag filings 12 months,
6 months, and 3 months before lapse. Many lenders use automated portfolio
management systems to track continuation deadlines.

### Q12: When must a secured party file a termination?

The secured party is **required** to file a UCC-3 Termination when the secured
obligation has been fully satisfied (the debt has been paid in full and there
are no outstanding obligations under the security agreement).

**Timing:**
- **Non-consumer transactions:** The secured party must file a termination
  within **20 days** of receiving an authenticated demand from the debtor
- **Consumer transactions:** The secured party must file a termination within
  **30 days** of the secured obligation being satisfied (no demand required)

**Penalties for failure to terminate:**
- The debtor may recover **$500 in statutory damages** under UCC 9-625(e)(4)
- The debtor may also recover **actual damages** caused by the failure to
  terminate (e.g., inability to obtain new financing)
- In some states, additional penalties or attorney's fees may be available

## Search and Due Diligence Questions

### Q13: How do I perform a proper UCC search?

1. **Determine the correct search state:**
   - For organizations: state of organization (not state of operations)
   - For individuals: state of principal residence

2. **Use the correct debtor name:**
   - For organizations: exact legal name from state business entity database
   - For individuals: name as shown on driver's license

3. **Choose your search method:**
   - Online portal: fastest, cheapest, self-service
   - Certified search: official certificate from the filing office (required
     for some transactions, e.g., title insurance)
   - Third-party service: convenient, often includes analysis

4. **Review results carefully:**
   - Verify debtor name matches your target
   - Check filing status (active, lapsed, terminated)
   - Read collateral descriptions
   - Review all related amendments (UCC-3s)
   - Note filing dates for priority analysis

5. **Search related states:**
   - If the debtor operates in multiple states, consider searching those
     states as well (though filings should be in the state of organization)
   - Search county records if fixtures are involved

### Q14: What is a "certified search" and when do I need one?

A certified search is an official search conducted by the filing office staff,
which produces a **certificate** attesting to the results. The certificate is
a legal document that can be relied upon.

You typically need a certified search for:
- Real estate closings involving personal property
- Commercial loan closings
- Due diligence for mergers and acquisitions
- Litigation (proving the existence or absence of liens)
- Title insurance underwriting

A certified search costs more ($15-$50 per name) and takes longer (1-5
business days) than an online search, but it carries legal weight.

### Q15: I found a UCC filing against my target debtor. How do I determine the lien position?

To determine lien position (first lien, second lien, etc.):

1. **Collect all active filings** against the debtor in the correct state
2. **Sort by file date** (earliest = highest priority, generally)
3. **Check for PMSI filings:** A purchase-money security interest has
   "super-priority" over earlier filings for the same collateral
4. **Review collateral descriptions:** Two filings may cover different
   collateral (no conflict)
5. **Check for subordination agreements:** A senior lender may have agreed to
   subordinate — this is NOT reflected in the UCC filing system; you need
   the actual agreement
6. **Check for federal tax liens:** IRS and state tax liens have special
   priority rules and may prime UCC filings
7. **Review assignments:** The current holder of the security interest may
   differ from the original filer
8. **Consider judgment liens:** State court judgments may also affect priority

### Q16: How do I interpret an "all assets" filing?

An "all assets" or "all personal property" collateral description in a UCC-1
means the secured party claims a security interest in essentially ALL of the
debtor's personal property assets. This includes:

- All accounts (receivables)
- All inventory
- All equipment
- All general intangibles (IP, goodwill, etc.)
- All deposit accounts (subject to control requirements)
- All chattel paper
- All instruments
- All investment property
- All proceeds of the above

**Implications:**
- This is a **blanket lien** — the strongest form of collateral coverage
- Any subsequent lender will be subordinate for the same collateral types
  (unless a subordination or intercreditor agreement exists)
- The debtor has limited ability to grant security interests to other lenders
  without consent
- In analysis, an "all assets" filing is often a sign of an asset-based
  lending (ABL) facility, a revolving credit line, or a term loan with
  comprehensive collateral coverage

**Caveats:**
- "All assets" in the FINANCING STATEMENT is valid, but "all assets" in the
  SECURITY AGREEMENT may not be sufficiently specific to "reasonably
  identify" the collateral (a more specific description is required in the
  security agreement itself)
- Deposit accounts require perfection by control, not filing — so even an
  "all assets" filing does not perfect a security interest in deposit accounts
