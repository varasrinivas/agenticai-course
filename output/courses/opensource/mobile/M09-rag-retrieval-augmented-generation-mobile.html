RAG — Retrieval-Augmented Generation

The model is brilliant but has a knowledge cutoff. RAG puts your documents on its desk at query time — no retraining. Seven concepts: the knowledge problem, the pipeline, embeddings, chunking, vector DBs, citations, and multimodal inputs.

Concept 1 of 7
The Big Idea

The model doesn't know your docs

Every model has a fixed knowledge cutoff — a date after which it saw no data. It also never saw your private company docs, your recent research, or your internal databases. Ask about any of it and the model hallucinates: confident, plausible, wrong.

Prompt engineering can't fix this. No matter how clever the wording, the model can't reference a document it has never seen. You have to hand it the source.

Concept 1 of 7
The Analogy

The doctor who stopped reading journals

BEFORE: Imagine a brilliant doctor who graduated in 2023 and has never opened a journal since — practicing entirely from what they learned in school.

PAIN: A patient asks about a breakthrough approved six months ago. The doctor either says "I don't know" or confidently recommends an outdated protocol — and the patient can't tell the difference.

MAPPING: That's the cutoff problem. The model is the doctor, your fresh docs are the new journals, and RAG is the system that drops the right journal on the desk exactly when it's needed.

Concept 1 of 7
How It Works

Why the gap exists

  1. Training freezesThe model learns from data up to a cutoff date, then stops. Nothing after that exists to it.
  2. Private data is invisibleYour internal docs were never in the training set — the model has zero access to them.
  3. Gaps get filled with guessesAsked about the unknown, the model produces fluent, plausible text that may be flatly wrong.
  4. RAG closes the gapRetrieve the relevant document at query time and paste it into the prompt — no retraining.
Concept 1 of 7
Decision Framework

RAG or fine-tune?

IF knowledge changes often
   or lives in private docs:
  → use RAG (retrieve at query time)

IF you need new behavior / style
   / a skill, not new facts:
  → consider fine-tuning

# RAG: minutes, ~free, no ML expertise
# fine-tune: weeks, $5k-$50k, retrain
# most doc problems = RAG

A support team cut hallucinated answers from ~35% to under 3% with a RAG prototype built in an afternoon.

Concept 1 of 7
Wrong Mental Models

Misconceptions

❌
"A bigger, newer model won't need RAG."
Every model still has a cutoff and still can't see your private data. Newer doesn't mean it knows your Q3 filings or last week's tickets.
❌
"Just fine-tune it on our docs."
Fine-tuning costs thousands, takes weeks, and must be redone whenever the docs change. RAG adds text at query time — update a document and retrieval reflects it instantly.

Models have a knowledge cutoff and no access to your private data, so they hallucinate on both. RAG fixes it by retrieving the source at query time.

Reach for RAG when the problem is missing facts; fine-tune only for missing behavior.

Concept 2 of 7
The Big Idea

Search, then generate

RAG is two phases. A one-time setup phase: chunk your docs, embed each chunk, store the vectors. Then a query phase that runs on every question: search for the most related chunks and paste them into the prompt so the model answers from real text.

The user never sees the machinery. They ask a question and get an accurate, sourced answer — because the model read actual retrieved chunks instead of guessing from memory.

Concept 2 of 7
The Analogy

Closed book vs open book

BEFORE: Picture a closed-book exam where you must answer purely from memory — you crammed for months, but thousands of facts simply wouldn't fit.

PAIN: Hit a topic you didn't study deeply and you either leave it blank or guess. Guessing confidently is exactly what a hallucination looks like.

MAPPING: RAG turns it into an open-book exam: the model is the student, your corpus is the textbook on the desk, and retrieval is the index at the back that flips to the right page instead of guessing.

Concept 2 of 7
How It Works

The six-stage pipeline

  1. Load & ChunkRead your documents and split them into small pieces (setup, once).
  2. Embed & StoreTurn each chunk into a vector and save it in a vector database (setup, once).
  3. RetrieveEmbed the user's question, search the store, take the top-K closest chunks.
  4. GeneratePaste those chunks into the prompt; the model answers and cites its sources.

Load → Chunk → Embed → Store → Retrieve → Generate

Concept 2 of 7
The Pattern

Pseudocode

# SETUP (once)
FOR each doc:
  chunks = split(doc)
  FOR each chunk:
    store(embed(chunk), chunk)

# QUERY (every question)
FUNCTION answer(question):
  q_vec = embed(question)
  top = store.search(q_vec, k=3)
  prompt = context(top) + question
  RETURN model.generate(prompt)
Concept 2 of 7
Wrong Mental Models

Misconceptions

❌
"RAG eliminates hallucinations."
It reduces them a lot (≈35% to under 3%), not to zero. The model can still misread context. Always add "answer ONLY from the context" and verify critical outputs.
❌
"More retrieved chunks = better answers."
Diminishing returns fast. 3–5 sharp chunks beat 20 noisy ones — too many flood the context and middle chunks get "lost in the middle."

RAG = search then generate: retrieve the top-K relevant chunks, paste them in, let the model answer from real text.

Setup runs once; the query phase runs every question. Retrieve few, relevant chunks — not many.

Concept 3 of 7
The Big Idea

Meaning becomes geometry

An embedding turns text into a list of numbers — a point in space — where the numbers encode what the text means, not which words it uses. Two sentences that say the same thing land at nearby points even with zero shared words.

"Cancel my subscription" and "I want to stop paying" end up close together. That's the whole trick: similar meaning becomes short distance, which is what makes semantic search possible.

Concept 3 of 7
The Analogy

A map of meaning

BEFORE: Old keyword search only finds exact words. Type "heart attack treatment" and it misses "myocardial infarction therapy" — same meaning, different words.

PAIN: Relevant results stay invisible unless the author used your exact vocabulary. You hit dead ends and never know what you missed.

MAPPING: Embeddings place text as coordinates on a map of meaning — "king" sits near "queen" like Paris sits near London. Cosine similarity measures the angle between two points: a small angle means similar meaning, whatever words were used.

Concept 3 of 7
How It Works

Text to vector to match

  1. An embedding model reads textA specialized network (not an LLM) outputs a fixed array of floats — typically 768–1,536 numbers.
  2. Meaning shapes the numbersTrained on millions of pairs, it places similar meanings at nearby coordinates.
  3. Compare by cosine similarityScore from 0 to 1; higher means closer meaning, regardless of length.
  4. Search by nearnessEmbed the query, find the nearest stored vectors — those are your best matches.
Concept 3 of 7
The Pattern

Pseudocode

v1 = embed("heart attack treatment")
v2 = embed("myocardial infarction therapy")
v3 = embed("best pizza recipes")

cosine(v1, v2)   # ~0.95  same meaning
cosine(v1, v3)   # ~0.10  unrelated

# nearness in space = similar meaning
# use the SAME model to index + query
Concept 3 of 7
Wrong Mental Models

Misconceptions

❌
"You can compare embeddings from different models."
You can't. Vectors from two different models live in different spaces — cosine similarity between them is meaningless. Use one model for both indexing and querying.
❌
"Embeddings understand language like people."
They capture statistical co-occurrence, not true understanding. "Bank" (money) and "bank" (river) collide unless context disambiguates — so embed a sentence, not a lone word.

Embeddings map text to points where similar meaning = short distance, enabling semantic search.

Always use the same embedding model to index and to query, and embed enough context to disambiguate.

Concept 4 of 7
The Big Idea

Split docs into searchable pieces

Chunking splits documents into small segments before embedding. Why not embed the whole doc? Because embedding models work best on short text, and a 5,000-word doc becomes a blurry average of everything — hard to match to a specific question.

The whole game is granularity. Too big and one relevant sentence drowns in noise; too small and a chunk loses the context that made it meaningful. Chunking quality directly sets retrieval quality.

Concept 4 of 7
The Analogy

Making study flashcards

BEFORE: You have a 300-page textbook and must turn it into study flashcards — but you decide how much text goes on each card up front.

PAIN: Too big (a whole chapter per card) and you re-read pages to find one fact. Too small (one sentence per card) and "the treatment was effective" is meaningless — which treatment, for what?

MAPPING: Chunking is that exact trade-off. Each chunk is a flashcard the retriever hands the model, and the right card size depends on how dense and structured your material is.

Concept 4 of 7
How It Works

Three strategies + overlap

  1. Fixed-sizeSplit by character or token count. Simple and predictable, but may cut mid-sentence.
  2. RecursiveSplit at natural boundaries first — headings, then paragraphs, then sentences.
  3. SemanticDetect topic shifts by comparing embedding similarity and split where the topic changes.
  4. Add overlapDuplicate 10–20% of text at each boundary so a sentence spanning two chunks isn't lost.
Concept 4 of 7
Decision Framework

Which chunking to use

IF content is uniform / plain text:
  → fixed-size (~300-500 chars,
     10-20% overlap) — start here

IF docs have clear structure
   (headings, sections):
  → recursive (respect boundaries)

IF topics shift mid-document
   and quality still lags:
  → semantic (topic-shift splits)

# always tune on YOUR retrieval quality
Concept 4 of 7
Wrong Mental Models

Misconceptions

❌
"Bigger chunks = better results."
Usually the opposite. A 2,000-word chunk with one relevant sentence dilutes the signal — the model wades through 1,999 irrelevant words. Smaller, focused chunks retrieve better.
❌
"Chunks shouldn't overlap — that's duplication."
Without overlap, a sentence on a boundary gets split and neither chunk answers the question. A little overlap is cheap insurance against lost context.

Chunking granularity directly determines retrieval quality — too big buries the signal, too small loses context.

Start with ~300–500 char chunks and overlap, then tune on real retrieval results for your content.

Concept 5 of 7
The Big Idea

Storage built for similarity search

A vector database does one job well: store high-dimensional vectors and find the closest matches to a query vector, fast. It also keeps the original text and metadata beside each vector, so a search returns the actual chunk — not just numbers.

You never touch the raw vectors. You hand it a question, it embeds, searches, and returns ranked chunks with their source. ChromaDB, Pinecone, and pgvector are common choices.

Concept 5 of 7
The Analogy

A library shelved by topic

BEFORE: A traditional library shelves books alphabetically by author. To find everything on "machine learning in healthcare," you'd need to know every author and check each shelf.

PAIN: You miss relevant books because you didn't know the author, and you waste hours browsing shelves organized by something you don't care about.

MAPPING: A vector DB shelves books by what they're about. Everything on that topic sits together regardless of author, and you find it by describing the topic — not by knowing an exact title.

Concept 5 of 7
How It Works

One row, then a query

  1. Each row has four fieldsAn id, the vector, the original document text, and metadata (source, index).
  2. The vector is what's searchedYour query gets embedded and compared by cosine similarity.
  3. ANN skips the full scanApproximate nearest neighbor (HNSW) checks ~200 vectors, not 100,000 — O(log n).
  4. Text + metadata come backYou get the top-K chunks to read, ranked by score, plus where they came from.
Concept 5 of 7
The Pattern

Pseudocode

# store: vector + text + metadata
db.add(
  id="chunk_14",
  vector=embed(text),
  document=text,
  metadata={ source: "guide.md" })

# query: DB embeds, ANN-searches, unpacks
hits = db.query("How long until lapse?", k=3)
FOR h IN hits:
  use(h.document, h.metadata, h.score)
Concept 5 of 7
Wrong Mental Models

Misconceptions

❌
"A vector DB replaces my SQL database."
No. Vector DBs are bad at exact lookups, joins, and transactions. Most production RAG uses both — a vector DB for retrieval, SQL for metadata and business logic.
❌
"More vectors = proportionally slower search."
HNSW scales logarithmically — 10K to 100K barely moves query time. The usual bottleneck is embedding the query, not the search.

A vector DB stores vector + text + metadata and returns the closest chunks fast via ANN (HNSW).

Prototype with ChromaDB locally; pair it with SQL for exact lookups and business data.

Concept 6 of 7
The Big Idea

Never render a citation you haven't verified

Retrieval gives you chunks and an answer, but tying each claim back to its source is your job. Managed models ship a native citations API; open-source models on Ollama don't — there's no citations[] array.

The open-source pattern is just as audit-grade because you own the step that actually matters: verification. Force the model to cite a chunk id + a verbatim quote, then confirm the quote really appears in that chunk before showing it.

Concept 6 of 7
The Analogy

The fact-checker at a newspaper

BEFORE: A reporter files a story full of confident quotes. Before it prints, someone has to make sure each quote is real.

PAIN: Print the reporter's "[1] [2]" references as-is and a fabricated or misremembered quote sails through — and in legal or medical work, an unsourced claim is one you can't defend.

MAPPING: Your verification code is the fact-checker. The model proposes a quote and a source; your code checks the quote is truly a substring of that chunk. Only then does the citation count.

Concept 6 of 7
How It Works

The citation contract

  1. Tag every chunkGive each retrieved chunk a stable id (C1, C2…) in the context you send.
  2. Demand structured citationsUse tool use to force the answer as claims, each with a chunk_id and a verbatim quote.
  3. Verify, never trustCheck the id exists and the quote is genuinely a substring of that chunk's text.
  4. Flag failuresAny claim that fails verification renders as UNVERIFIED — surfaced, not silently trusted.
Concept 6 of 7
The Pattern

Pseudocode

# force claims: {text, chunk_id, quote}
claims = model.answer_with_citations(
           question, tagged_chunks)

FOR claim IN claims:
  src = chunks[claim.chunk_id]
  claim.verified = src AND
    norm(claim.quote) IN norm(src)

# normalize case + whitespace first —
# models re-space quotes.
# show UNVERIFIED, don't hide it.
Concept 6 of 7
Wrong Mental Models

Misconceptions

❌
"Just ask for [1] [2] refs and display them."
The model can fabricate the numbers. Displaying unverified references is the classic anti-pattern — require a chunk id + verbatim quote and confirm both before rendering.
❌
"Open models can't do audit-grade citations."
They can. The verification pass is what makes provenance trustworthy, and it's code you own — it works on any chat model, hosted or local, with no vendor lock-in.

Provenance is verify-then-render: force chunk id + verbatim quote per claim, confirm it against the source, flag what fails.

Use chunk attribution for internal tools; add verification the moment a wrong, unsourced claim has real consequences.

Concept 7 of 7
The Big Idea

Everything becomes text first

Real documents arrive as PDFs, scans, charts, and screenshots — not clean text. Hosted models can read a PDF natively; a text-only local model like Mistral can't. There's no "just send the PDF" shortcut.

So you convert everything to text upstream, then feed it into the exact RAG pipeline you already built. Digital PDFs get their text extracted, scans get OCR, and pixels-with-meaning go through a vision model that describes them.

Concept 7 of 7
The Analogy

The translator at the front desk

BEFORE: Imagine a brilliant analyst who only reads English, but documents arrive in French, as handwriting, and as photographs of whiteboards.

PAIN: Hand any of those straight to the analyst and they're stuck — they can't process a format they don't read, so the document is effectively invisible.

MAPPING: You put a translator at the front desk. Each format gets converted to plain English before it reaches the analyst: extractor for digital PDFs, OCR for scans, a vision model for pictures. The analyst only ever sees text.

Concept 7 of 7
How It Works

Three conversion tracks

  1. Digital PDFHas a text layer — pull prose and tables with pdfplumber or PyMuPDF.
  2. Scanned pageJust an image of text — run OCR (Tesseract) at 300 DPI to recover it.
  3. Chart / photoNo text to extract — a vision model (LLaVA, Llama Vision) describes it as text.
  4. Converge to text → RAGAll three produce plain text that chunks, embeds, and retrieves unchanged.
Concept 7 of 7
Decision Framework

Route each format

IF PDF has a text layer:
  → pdfplumber / PyMuPDF (near free)

IF scanned page or photo of text:
  → Tesseract OCR (rasterize @300 DPI)

IF chart, diagram, or photo:
  → vision model (LLaVA), cache result

IF unsure a PDF is scanned:
  → try extract_text(); empty? → OCR

Vision models are heavy — reserve them for genuine pixels-with-meaning and cache the text description.

Concept 7 of 7
Wrong Mental Models

Misconceptions

❌
"A text-only model can just take a PDF."
It can't — there's no document or image block for Mistral on Ollama. You must extract text first, then feed that into RAG like any other document.
❌
"Run every page through the vision model."
Vision models need multi-GB of VRAM and run slow. Use extraction or OCR when there's real text; save the vision model for charts and photos, and cache its output.

On text-only local models, convert every format to text upstream — extract, OCR, or vision — then reuse your RAG pipeline unchanged.

Route by type at ingest, cache expensive vision results, and keep every document on your own hardware.

Quick Quiz

Test the core insight

One question per concept. Tap to reveal the answer.

Why can't prompt engineering alone fix the knowledge problem?
Tap to reveal ↓
The model can't reference what it never saw. Its training has a cutoff and it has no access to your private data. No wording makes it cite a document that isn't in front of it — you have to retrieve and inject the source. RAG does that; fine-tuning is for behavior, not fresh facts.
What are the two phases of a RAG system?
Tap to reveal ↓
A one-time setup phase and a per-query phase. Setup: chunk, embed, and store your docs. Query: embed the question, retrieve the top-K closest chunks, paste them into the prompt, and generate. Search, then generate — retrieve few, relevant chunks, not many.
Why does keyword search miss results that embeddings catch?
Tap to reveal ↓
Keywords need exact word matches; embeddings compare meaning. "Heart attack treatment" won't match "myocardial infarction therapy" by keyword, but their embeddings land close together. Similar meaning becomes short distance — just use the same embedding model to index and query.
Why can bigger chunks hurt retrieval quality?
Tap to reveal ↓
They dilute the signal. A large chunk with one relevant sentence forces the model through walls of irrelevant text, and its embedding becomes a blurry average. Smaller, focused chunks (with a little overlap) match specific questions far better.
How does a vector DB search millions of vectors in milliseconds?
Tap to reveal ↓
Approximate nearest neighbor (ANN), usually HNSW. Instead of comparing the query against every vector, it navigates a layered graph and checks a few hundred — O(log n) with 95–99% accuracy. That's why 10K vs 100K vectors barely changes query time.
What makes an open-source citation audit-grade without a native API?
Tap to reveal ↓
Your verification pass. Force each claim to carry a chunk id + a verbatim quote, then confirm the quote is truly a substring of that chunk before rendering. Anything unverifiable is flagged, not shown. Never render a citation you haven't verified.
How do you feed a PDF or chart to a text-only local model?
Tap to reveal ↓
Convert everything to text upstream first. Digital PDFs → extract with pdfplumber; scans → Tesseract OCR; charts and photos → a vision model that describes them. All three produce plain text that flows into your existing RAG pipeline unchanged. Cache the expensive vision results.
